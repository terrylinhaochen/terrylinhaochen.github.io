## Growth depends on how knowledge is treated

Mokyr argues that cultural changes in early modern Europe helped create conditions for sustained scientific and technological progress. The focus is not simply on having clever people, but on an environment in which new knowledge could be proposed, contested, and used.

## Trace a mechanism rather than a mood

When the narrative invokes culture, ask what people actually did differently. Who could challenge an accepted claim? How did ideas circulate? What made useful knowledge worth pursuing? These questions help keep the explanation connected to institutions and behavior rather than a vague label of national character.

## Compare without assuming inevitability

The argument concerns a particular historical development, not a timeless ranking of societies. Read it alongside explanations emphasizing resources, politics, and economic incentives. For an organization today, the relevant question is modest: what practices make criticism, experimentation, and exchange possible? That application is an analogy to test, not proof that a workplace can reproduce an intellectual revolution.
