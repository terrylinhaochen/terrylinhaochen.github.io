## Integration creates political choices

Rodrik challenges the idea that more global economic integration is always the right policy target. His central tension concerns democracy, national self-determination, and extensive economic globalization. Pursuing all three without limits can force a conflict over who gets to choose the rules.

## Ask who can change the rules

A useful way to apply this framing is to examine one policy disagreement. Which decisions are being standardized internationally? Which domestic choices become harder to reverse? Who benefits from predictability, and who loses the ability to adapt a rule to local preferences? These are analytical questions, not evidence that every international agreement is harmful.

## Distinguish globalization from its most demanding version

Rodrik’s argument is not a demand to end trade. It questions a model in which domestic institutions are continually reorganized around deeper integration. A more restrained international order can leave room for democratic variation. The practical reading task is to identify the tradeoff a proposal hides: do its promised economic gains require giving up a choice people still expect to make politically? Treat that tradeoff as something to debate openly, not a technical detail that experts have already settled.
